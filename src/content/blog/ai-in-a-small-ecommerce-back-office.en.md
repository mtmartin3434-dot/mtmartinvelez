For the last five years I have run customer care, email marketing and order admin for a small
skincare company. It is not a big operation. It is me, part-time, plus a handful of suppliers.
There is no customer service team, no marketing department, and no one to hand a task to.

That shape is exactly why AI became interesting to me. When you are the only person in the
function, the bottleneck is not decision-making — it is the sheer volume of small writing,
sorting and reformatting work that sits in front of you every day.

## Where it genuinely saves me time

**Turning messy emails into product answers.** Customers write in with symptoms, not product
numbers. "My face is flaking after I started your cream." Sorting those threads into a real
diagnosis, checking which of the five products in the range it relates to, and drafting a reply
that covers both the product advice and the reassurance is a twenty-minute job per email. I use AI
to structure the information and draft a first response, and then I write the actual reply myself.

**Email campaigns.** Writing, rescheduling, and adapting a campaign for a segment of the list.
The structure and the variant copy are a reasonable first pass; the judgement about what this
particular customer segment needs is mine.

**Reformatting everything else.** Order queries that need a summary for the account, product
information files, supplier emails in slightly formal English. The boring translation work of
taking what is in my head and putting it into a form someone else needs.

## What I would not hand over

The customers who arrive with a reaction. Someone whose skin is burning wants to hear from a
person who has seen the same thing a hundred times, not a well-written template. That is where
the trust in a small skincare brand comes from, and I am not willing to spend it on efficiency.

Nor the compliance work. Product information files and EU product notifications need accuracy
more than they need speed.

## Why I am writing this down

I have started studying properly — through Google and Coursera — because I could see that the
tools were getting better faster than I was learning to use them well. I got into this the
scrappy way, and it turns out that works fine until it doesn't.

The part nobody warns you about: it is very easy to become the person who has tried twelve AI
tools and can describe all of them. I would rather be the person who uses a small number well,
in a specific business, and can say what it saved and what it cost. If you are in the same
position — one person, too many small jobs, a real business behind them — I would be interested
in hearing what you have found useful.
